# Reading scientific literature

## Friday, September 11, 2026

Today’s focus is not simply finding a paper. Scientific papers are contributions to an ongoing conversation: they build on earlier work, support or qualify other claims, and become evidence for later papers.

## 60-minute plan

### 1. Pretest — 10 minutes

Complete the Microsoft Forms pretest. It checks basic source types, access, backward/forward searching, citation relationships, substructure searching, synthesis for a review, and when to stop searching. Use **Unsure** when appropriate; this is diagnostic, not graded.

### 2. Access sequence — 5 minutes

For each article, work through:

1. DOI or exact title
2. Publisher page
3. Mount Union library/Primo
4. Relevant database
5. Author or repository copy
6. OhioLINK/interlibrary loan

Record where the PDF actually came from.

### 3. Short close reading — 15 minutes

Read the abstract, introduction, selected figures, and conclusion of:

Ruangsupapichat, N.; Pollard, M. M.; Harutyunyan, S. R.; Feringa, B. L. “Reversing the direction in a light-driven rotary molecular motor.” *Nature Chemistry* **3**, 53–60 (2011). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nchem.872)

Ask:

- What question is the paper answering?
- What is its central claim?
- What chemical event allows reversal of direction?
- Which figure gives the strongest evidence?
- What remains uncertain or outside the scope of the paper?

### 4. Find the paper in conversation — 15 minutes

Compare the primary paper with:

Cantrill, S. “One-way traffic.” *Nature Chemistry* **3**, 907 (2011). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nchem.1215)

Use SciFinder or Web of Science to inspect references and citing papers. Classify each connection as:

- background;
- foundational earlier work;
- method or material borrowed;
- direct extension;
- new application;
- disagreement or qualification;
- general field-context citation.

### 5. Categorize sample articles and connect to the literature review — 15 minutes

Open the four sample articles already provided on the course site:

- JACS article
- Biochemistry article
- Nature Commentary
- ACS Chemical Neuroscience: “What are people reading?”

For each item, classify it as **primary research**, **review**, **perspective/commentary/News & Views**, **other/unclear**, or **unsure**. Use the citation, article page, and abstract/opening text—not just the journal name. Discuss what each source would be useful for in a literature review.

Complete one row:

| Paper | Question/claim | Evidence | Earlier work | Later response | Role in my review |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

The “why” of a literature review often emerges during searching. Repeated methods, disagreements, gaps, and landmark papers help define the question.

## AI and literature searching

AI may help generate background vocabulary, explain unfamiliar concepts, suggest search terms, organize a draft, or provide writing feedback. It does not replace database searching, citation chaining, or checking the actual paper. If AI suggests a field overview or landmark papers, use those suggestions as a starting map and verify them in the literature.
