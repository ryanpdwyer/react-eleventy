# Finding papers and following the conversation

This page is a short set of guided examples. The point is to practice both **getting the paper** and asking what the paper is doing in the field.

## Follow the access trail

For each example, begin with the citation or DOI and get the full text however you can. Record where it actually came from.

### Citation-relationship prompts

- Is the citation being used as background, evidence, a method, a comparison, or a disagreement?
- Does the later author actually test the earlier claim, or merely mention it?
- What changed between the two papers: molecule, method, conditions, measurement, interpretation, or application?
- If the papers disagree, is the disagreement about data, analysis, definitions, or scope?
- What search should come next: an earlier reference, a citing paper, a related-citation search, or a new keyword search?

### Components of a literature review

1. Define a provisional question.
2. Find useful vocabulary and a few seed papers.
3. Identify foundational work and major methods.
4. Follow backward and forward citations.
5. Group papers by claim, method, result, or disagreement—not only by author or year.
6. Compare evidence and limitations.
7. Decide what role each paper plays in the review.
8. Stop when new searches mostly repeat the same papers and ideas.
